1. Executive summary
The business consists of multiple family-managed companies operating in renovation, construction and related services. The husband manages the operational side, including site work, subcontractors, materials and day-to-day decisions. The wife manages the back office, including client relationships, quotations, invoices, supplier bills, supplier price information and administrative control.
The current operating model is highly dependent on the owners, fragmented documents and manual knowledge. A key operational process, preparing quotations, is still performed on paper by the husband. Supplier information is distributed across different portals, invoices, historical purchases and company-specific discounted price lists. This creates duplicated work, slow quotation preparation, pricing risk and limited visibility of actual project profitability and cash requirements.
The business needs a single, simple and reliable management environment tailored to renovation work. It must cover the complete lifecycle from the first customer contact and site visit through quotation, acceptance, project execution, purchasing, invoicing, collections, supplier payments and final margin analysis. It must also recognise that these are several companies, not one business entity, while still allowing the owners to see a consolidated view.
Core requirement
Create one source of truth that is simple enough for a very non-digital operational user, while providing the back office with structured control over customers, suppliers, quotations, project costs, invoices, payments and margins.
1.1 Confirmed needs
Support more than one company or legal entity, with separate records and consolidated owner visibility.
Support two primary user profiles with very different working styles, operational and back-office.
Replace paper-based quotation preparation with a guided, fast and low-friction process.
Maintain structured catalogues of work items, materials, services, labour and machinery.
Maintain multiple supplier price lists, negotiated discounts and purchase history.
Compare alternative prices by supplier or subcontractor at item, chapter and project level.
Control quote versions, optional items, accepted scope and subsequent changes.
Convert accepted quotations into projects without re-entering information.
Track real purchasing, labour, subcontracting and other costs against the accepted budget.
Manage customer invoices, collections, supplier bills, partial payments and cash flow.
Provide clear management visibility of timing, pending actions, margin and cash exposure.
1.2 Requirements principles
Principle
| Meaning for the business
Ease before sophistication
| The operational user must be able to use the core process with minimal typing, minimal screens and clear prompts.
Capture once, reuse everywhere
| Customer, project, scope, quantity and price information should not be re-entered in multiple files.
Traceability
| Every quote, price, purchase, invoice, payment and change must have a clear origin, date and responsible person.
Real profitability
| Commercial margin must be compared with actual cost and actual cash movement, not only estimated cost.
Multi-company control
| Transactions must belong to the correct company while management can view combined performance.
Incremental adoption
| The first usable process should solve quotation and project control pain without requiring all future functions on day one.

2.2 Multi-company requirement
The owners manage several companies. The management process must therefore distinguish company-specific data from shared business data. The exact legal structure must be validated, but the following requirements apply regardless of the final technology:
Every client, quote, project, purchase, supplier bill, customer invoice, payment and receipt must identify the relevant company.
Each company must be able to use its own legal details, invoice numbering, bank details, tax information and document templates.
Shared supplier, material and service information should be reusable across companies where appropriate, without losing company-specific discounts or terms.
The owners require both individual-company reporting and a consolidated management view.
Intercompany purchases, labour sharing, machinery use or recharges must be identifiable if they occur.

3. Current-state processes and pain points
Process
| Observed current state
| Main pain / risk
Customer enquiry
| Customer information and follow-up may be spread across phone, messaging, email and personal knowledge.
| Leads can be forgotten, communication history is incomplete and information is duplicated.
Site visit and scope capture
| The husband assesses the job and records information informally or on paper.
| Measurements, decisions, photos and assumptions may not be consistently transferred to the quotation.
Quotation preparation
| The husband prepares quotations on paper. The wife performs back-office processing and document preparation.
| Slow turnaround, repeated typing, transcription errors, limited version control and high dependency on both owners.
Pricing
| Prices are obtained from several suppliers, their personal portals, past invoices, past purchases and discounted price lists.
| No single current price source, difficult comparison, expired prices and weak evidence for selected costs.
Supplier / subcontractor comparison
| Comparisons are built manually in spreadsheets for individual projects.
| Useful analysis exists but is labour-intensive, project-specific and difficult to reuse.
Project conversion
| Accepted quotes may require information to be recreated in separate tracking files.
| Loss of the original commercial baseline and duplicated setup work.
Purchasing and deliveries
| Purchases, invoices and supplier records are controlled in separate documents or portals.
| Limited visibility of ordered, delivered, returned, invoiced and paid amounts by project.
Execution control
| Operational progress and issues depend heavily on the husband and informal communication.
| No consistent progress record, difficult coordination and weak early warning of delays or cost overruns.
Customer invoicing and collection
| Invoices and payments are handled by the wife, potentially on an ad hoc basis.
| Manual follow-up, unclear outstanding amounts, partial payment allocation and weak project cash visibility.
Supplier bills and payments
| Bills and payment dates vary by supplier and may be manually recorded.
| Risk of missed due dates, duplicate payments, poor cash forecasting and incomplete project cost allocation.
Profitability analysis
| Estimated supplier alternatives may be compared, but actual project costs are not fully integrated with revenue and collections.
| The owners may know turnover but not the final real margin, cash contribution or cause of variance.

7.1 Price data requirements
Prices must be dated and linked to a supplier or other source.
The same item may have several simultaneous supplier prices.
Company-specific discounts and terms must be retained.
Historical quote and project prices must not change when a current price list is updated.
Prices must distinguish net amount, VAT, transport, waste, minimum order or other applicable charges where relevant.
A price should have an expiry or review date when known.
Supplier product codes and internal catalogue codes must be mappable without requiring them to be identical.
7.2 Quote and project hierarchy
Required hierarchy
Company > Customer > Site / Opportunity > Quotation > Version > Chapter > Subchapter > Work item > Resource / supplier price. After acceptance, the accepted version becomes the project baseline and is supplemented by purchases, labour, bills, invoices, payments and approved changes.
7.3 Migration and data quality
Existing customer, supplier, cost, quotation and project spreadsheets should be reviewed and mapped before migration.
Duplicate customers and suppliers must be identified and resolved.
Old prices must not be presented as current without their source date.
Imported codes, descriptions and units should be normalised while preserving the original source reference.
Incomplete historical projects may be imported at summary level if detailed reconstruction is not economically justified.
The owners should agree who is responsible for approving master-data changes.

11.3 Quotation process

11.4 Suppliers and prices
